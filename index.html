Rogue Waves in Photorefractive Media

Since ancient times, sailors have told stories of mermaids and sea monsters that were said to lure or even abduct seafarers, reflecting the mysteries and dangers of the open ocean. What was once considered myth is now understood scientifically as real phenomena in the form of extreme waves, known as rogue waves. These waves are not limited to the ocean but also appear in diverse physical systems such as optics, plasmas, superfluids, the atmosphere, and Bose–Einstein condensates. Rogue waves emerge suddenly, reach extraordinary amplitudes—often more than twice the significant wave height—and vanish just as quickly, making them both fascinating and difficult to study. Closely related phenomena, called breathers, are localized oscillatory wave structures that play a key role in understanding rogue wave dynamics, as they often arise from modulational instability and can evolve into extreme waves under suitable conditions.

Recently, we have investigated extreme wave phenomena in photorefractive media, focusing on SBN:60 crystals, where the injected laser power remains relatively low. Although extreme events such as rogue waves have not yet been observed experimentally under these conditions, our theoretical analysis indicates that their occurrence is possible. We propose NLSE-NLSE transformations to solve the relevant nonlinear evolution equations, obtaining exact Akhmediev breather (AB) solutions, which then serve as a foundation for constructing first-order rogue wave solutions in these media. In addition, we also discovered new spatial soliton solutions—steering solitons—offering a more straightforward and efficient approach to controlling nonlinear wave propagation in photorefractive media. Optimizing Quantum Reflection of Bose-Einstein Condensate

Imagine throwing a volleyball at a wall—it bounces back after hitting the surface. However, the quantum reflection of a Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC) behaves differently. A BEC, composed of atoms such as 23Na, can reflect off a solid surface, like planar silicon, even before touching it, as if an invisible force or “ghost” is pushing it away. This fascinating phenomenon was first observed by a team at MIT in 2004 and remains a captivating mystery. Interestingly, the reflection probability of BECs decreases as the incident velocity gets lower, contradicting conventional quantum mechanical predictions. Scientists suggest that interatomic interactions within the BEC trigger dynamic excitations, such as solitons and vortex rings, which fragment the condensate and reduce the reflection probability.

In 2024, we collaborated with Universiti Malaysia Perlis (UNIMAP) to propose a novel idea for optimizing the quantum reflection of BECs. This study emphasizes the influence of the incidence angle of the BEC on a planar surface. The results indicate that by adjusting the angle of incidence, the quantum reflection can be optimized: reflection probability increases, while saturation effects or anomalous behaviors are suppressed. Off-Shooting Solitons

Have you ever heard of beams that can self-accelerate, self-heal, and resist diffraction? These extraordinary beams are known as Airy beams. Originally, they appeared as a solution to the free-potential Schrödinger equation in quantum mechanics, discovered by Berry & Balazs (1979). A paradigm shift occurred in 2007 when Siviloglou et al. demonstrated that Airy beams can propagate along a curved trajectory in optical media, which was experimentally confirmed in the same year. Today, optical Airy beams are used in a variety of applications, from microparticle manipulation to light-sheet microscopy, and have become a prominent topic of research.

In recent years, researchers have explored how Airy beams behave in nonlinear optical media, particularly photorefractive materials. Experiments by Bouchet et al., 2018, showed that under focusing nonlinear conditions, the Airy beam may split into a weakly accelerating component and a structure termed an “off-shooting soliton” (OSS), which propagates through the medium without transverse acceleration. Our theoretical study suggests that OSS exhibit characteristics identical to conventional spatial solitons. We hope this finding will engage the scientific community, especially experimentalists, in exploring the rich dynamics and applications of spatial solitons. Diffusion-Trapped Airy Beams

It may sound unusual, but the Airy beam is known as a light beam capable of accelerating itself, self-healing, curving during propagation, and even maintaining its shape against diffraction. However, what happens if this beam propagates in a nonlinear medium influenced by diffusive thermal energy? In 2024, for the first time, this phenomenon was analyzed using the time-dependent diffusive-saturable nonlinear Schrödinger equation (DSNLSE), considered within an unbiased photorefractive medium (BaTiO3). The study revealed that illumination time plays a crucial role in controlling the propagation of the Airy beam. Over long propagation distances, the competition between photorefractive and diffusive nonlinearities gives rise to unstable deflecting soliton beams. In contrast, at shorter distances, the Airy beam is still able to maintain its acceleration along a certain trajectory as time progresses.

This study also uncovers how interactions between Airy beams are influenced by phase and time. When they are in phase, their behavior changes with exposure time, while out-of-phase beams exhibit almost no significant variations. Interestingly, fusion solitons naturally emerge under in-phase conditions. Furthermore, it was found that these self-trapped wave packets undergo bending during propagation, with an acceleration rate dependent on thermal energy associated with diffusive nonlinearity. The findings are consistent with previous experiments. Full details of the results can be found in the published article below.
